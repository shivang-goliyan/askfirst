The failure, measured twice — once by someone else, once by us

Offered a way out, a frontier model takes it one time in five.

Take 500 clinical questions, delete the correct option, and put "insufficient information" on the page instead. GPT-5 abstains 19.2% of the time and picks one of the remaining wrong answers for the other 80.8%, at a mean confidence of 0.69.

Newman-Toker is elsewhere in this deck; this is arXiv 2608.09080, Table IV, on MedMCQA. We checked the table rather than the abstract.

So we deleted four fifths of a patient record and asked an open model again. Its confidence fell 12%. A classifier fitted to the same data lost 83% of its confidence on the same deletion.

It still ranks its own answers well — its most confident thirtieth is 93.3% right against 35.3% overall. What it cannot do is notice the batch got harder, so a cut-off set on complete records to deliver 80% delivers 54.7% at two fifths. That gap is the product.

What it is

Not a diagnosis tool. A gate in front of the model that decides.

It answers a narrower and far more answerable question: is there enough here?

  1. Record split into atomic fields.
  2. Open model scores it — the full distribution is kept, not a sampled answer.
  3. Each present field removed in turn → what the answer rests on.
  4. Each absent field ranked by how much it could tell you, then by cost and minutes.
  5. Too little record, or not confident enough for the bar you set → refuse, return the ranked gaps, hand over. The ablation above is what the page shows you about the answer; it is not what makes the call.

That is a setting, not a slogan. Answer 98% of complete records, 35% at two fifths, and none at all once the record is thin enough that referring everyone is cheaper than any coverage the grid contains. Our grid had no zero column until a review pointed out that 5.24 is not the cheapest number when blanket referral costs 5.00.

Seven price ratios at 20% evidence: 28% to 98% cheaper. Even when a referral costs half a wrong answer it is 28% cheaper. The direction does not depend on flattering prices.

The hole in that, and the fix · 30,000 held-out records

A setting is no use if it has to be set by the one person who cannot know it.

The scorer's confidence is honest on a full record and forty points underconfident at 40% evidence. A cut-off chosen on complete records promises 95% and delivers 48% on a tenth of one.

Two pieces, because one measured insufficient. Calibrate against findings in hand — observable, where the fraction is not. Then estimate how much of the record is missing (mean absolute error 0.057) and refuse below a floor whatever the confidence says.

Tested against seven ways a record goes short, not just the convenient one: the promise is tested in 26 of the 35 cells — the other 9 go silent, which is the floor working rather than a promise kept — and 24 of 26 clear it. The two that do not answered 19 cases and 1 case, so neither carries weight.

The job the open model actually wins · 100 held-out records

Prose in, findings out — and what it misses turns into caution, not mistakes.

The engine scores structured findings. The world produces prose. An open model reads one into the other: 72% of findings recovered, 87% of what it returns really there, 100 notes in 70 seconds. It is wrong often enough to matter — which is the point.

Ten cases stopped being answered. Three started. No wrong answer was introduced. What the reader misses makes the record look thinner, and a thinner record is exactly what the gate refuses.

It works because the gate reads the record, not the scorer's mood. The note was written by the same model that read it back, so both numbers are upper bounds.

Why this needs an open model, on AMD

The product is built on the distribution. A closed API returns text.

Entropy, information gain and a calibrated threshold are all functions of the full posterior. No version of this works behind an API that hands back a sentence. Nothing is attached today — everything on this page was measured on CPU.

We tested the obvious claim — that a bigger model makes more cases answerable — and it is false here.

300 patients, 44,100 scored prompts on a rented GPU: a 3B open model gets 35.3% on a complete record against our linear model's 99.6%. Take four fifths of the record away and its confidence barely moves — margin down 12%, ours down 83%. It still ranks its own answers well; what it cannot do is notice the batch got harder. A cut-off set on complete records to deliver 80% delivers 54.7% at 40% evidence.

What survives is measured: ESM-2 650M changes the safety conclusion the laptop-sized model gets wrong — 4 of 28 calls move at supporting strength, not 0 of 11.

What we got wrong, with dates on it · 1 of 2

The biggest thing we got wrong, we found ourselves.

  • We published a headline that measured our own baseline, then killed it. "21% to 85.3% by refusing" came from a naive Bayes at 22.6% top-1. A linear model gets 99.6% in 31 seconds on a laptop, so the lift was about our classifier, not about medicine. Found by two of our own files disagreeing, re-run end to end, old notes kept with a notice on them.
  • A French-to-English label join was broken — the conditions file is keyed in French, the patient labels are English, overlap 1 of 42. Caught before the GPU run, which would have scored French predictions against English truth at $1.99 an hour.
  • Three bugs in our own ranking measurement, every one in our favour. A negative answer was switching the evidence feature on; the ranker assumed every question gets answered; and max() was breaking a three-way tie using the engine's own sort order, worth seven points to us. Fixing the first two made the published method we beat worse, not better.
  • The word "calibrated" was doing work it had not earned, so we checked it. The confidence is honest on a full record and forty points underconfident at 40% evidence.
Every failure is dated and committed in docs/results/, in the order it happened. Nothing here was found by a judge.
What we got wrong, with dates on it · 2 of 2

A cheap property of the question beats the principled objective — by three points, not thirty.

what we ranked questions byscoreagainst
asking nothing at all77.8%78.5% random
expected information gain — the literature's, implemented faithfully85.5%77.8% silence
breadth × answer rate — what we ship88.8%90.1% oracle

We published this as "information gain loses to a coin and then to silence" — 41.3% against 43.7%. That is retracted. It was an artifact of a scorer that had never seen a thin record, so every entropy computed from its posterior was wrong on exactly the sparse cases where advice matters. Refitting the scorer on records with holes moved information gain from 41.3% to 85.5%. The measurement was broken, not the objective.

And our central assumption was that records go missing at random. They do not. Cut the same number of findings seven realistic ways and accuracy at a fifth of a record runs from 8.9% to 96.4% depending only on which fifth. Our refusal rule broke on the likeliest pattern of all and we could not fully fix it: fitting against that pattern and adding a feature for how unusual the findings are takes it from 91.5% to 93.6%, still short of the 95% it promises. The first version of that ladder said we had fixed it — it came from a scratch script, and when we made it reproducible the effect vanished.

Prior work — said by us, before it is said to us

MediQ posed the interactive version of this first. We are not claiming the idea.

  • MediQ, NeurIPS 2024, arXiv 2406.00922 — naive prompting to ask questions lowered accuracy by 11.3%. Their self-consistency fix gave +22.3%, leaving a 10.3% gap against full information. That is the number to beat, and it is a real target rather than a vague one.
  • Q4Dx, Nature Scientific Reports 2026 — an evaluation built by systematically removing information from cases. That is our evaluation design, already published.
  • ALFA (arXiv 2502.14860), BALAR / MedClarify — expected information gain over a fixed question set.

What we have not found occupied: ranking the absent fields by counterfactual influence over an open model's own logprobs. Absence of search evidence is not proof, and we say so rather than claiming a gap.

The same engine, a second domain

Variant interpretation, where the evidence contradicts itself.

CIViC, CC0, 4,940 evidence rows. 182 contested groups where the same knowledge base both supports and does not support the conclusion.

An ESM-2 score enters as one removable item, strength on a dial. At supporting, 0 of 11 calls change; at clinical, 5 of 11 — EGFR S768I on erlotinib goes from documented Sensitivity/Response to "predicted damaging".

What that is and is not. The vote is ours, and "Resistance" and "predicted damaging" are not answers to the same question. This is an aggregation rule failing when an over-weighted predictor enters it — not clinical calls being overturned.

And it is model-dependent: with ESM-2 650M, supporting strength moves 4 of 28, not 0 of 11. The cap held because the small model was unsure, not because the cap was sufficient.

Eleven groups is a demonstration, not a headline, and all eleven are on the page. 171 of 182 contested groups cannot take a computational vote at all with the 35M model — a number that itself shrinks as the model gets better.

Who pays · prices from the literature, not from us

We had been pricing a wrong answer at twenty handovers. It is nearer two hundred.

A triage call costs C$10–27 (CCOHTA 2003). A wrong answer costs P(harm) × what a harm costs: 4.4% against 11.1% error on dangerous presentations (Newman-Toker, BMJ Qual Saf 2024) × $422,000 average paid claim (CRICO). Ratio: 211:1 to 8,356:1. Every saving in this deck was computed at 20:1, so all of them are conservative.

Carried honestly, cost minimisation says switch the product off — above 4,000:1 the cheapest policy answers nothing. That is a flaw in the objective: clinicians are finite and the queue has to go somewhere.

The business, in numbers we measured ourselves

Thousands of dollars of clinician time returned per thousand records. $0.0004 to run.

The buyer is an operator whose cost line is clinician-minutes: a nurse triage line, a teleconsultation platform, the symptom-checker vendors selling into them. They already have the cost, already have a person for a refusal to reach, and already carry the liability.

never reach a person455

Price it at C$500 per thousand: a 7–19× return for them, and a gross margin a rounding error away from 100% at every volume.

And they cannot take it with them. The calibration has to be fitted to their missingness — measured at 1.1 points, intervals overlapping. The moat came out of a limitation rather than a roadmap. Nobody has bought it, there is no pilot, and the throughput is measured on synthetic patients. That is the gap.
